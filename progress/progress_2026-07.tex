\section{Overview}
This checkpoint catches the team up from the base paper to our current state. The arc: starting from
\citet{fein2026onebias} (spurious biases, the difference-of-means probe $+$ null-space projection, and the
low- vs high-complexity question), we ask the \emph{same} mechanistic question at a \emph{new locus} --- the
\rewardmodel{}, the shared upstream evaluator reused across RLHF, best-of-$N$, and data filtering --- along a
\emph{new axis}, demographic and intersectional protected-attribute bias. We built a two-domain matched-pair
pipeline; the bias is real, large, and behaviourally \emph{linearly} removable; but modelling the actual
model \emph{response} shows the RM rewards \textbf{correct reasoning}, not fairness; and mechanistically the
concepts are \textbf{entangled} --- linearly erasable yet non-linearly recoverable, so nulling fixes the
\emph{reward}, not the \emph{representation}.

Two scope decisions were taken this cycle (detailed in \S\ref{sec:decisions}): we are going \textbf{all-in
on Direction~1} (candidate Directions 2--5 parked), and we \textbf{added a third domain, education
(grading student assignments/tests)}, alongside credit and recruitment (CV) --- now built and run on real
essay corpora (see Results).

\section{Framing and the gap}
Reward models are the most reused and least directly observed component of the alignment stack, so a
systematic error there propagates across every downstream use of the reward signal. The regulatory hook is
the EU AI Act's Annex-III high-risk regime \citep{euaiact2024}: recruitment and creditworthiness are named
high-risk uses, and sex, age, and pregnancy/family status are protected. Prior fairness work debiases the
\emph{decision} model; the reward-model locus is the gap we occupy, and we carry the base paper's mechanistic
low- vs high-complexity lens into the protected-attribute setting.

\section{Approach --- design grammar}
Matched-pair contrastive design (hold content fixed, vary only the target attribute). Two metrics:
\autoinf{} (preference shift on equal-quality items) and \crossinf{} (reliability harm --- accuracy
degradation when a cue is attached to the weaker option; the metric that maps onto the Act's concern with
biased false positives/negatives). Each attribute is tested in an \textbf{explicit} form (stated) and a
\textbf{proxy} form (inferred from realistic cues). The mechanistic step: extract a \diffmean{} direction,
sweep null-space projection strength, and classify the bias low- vs high-complexity. Anchor case for credit
and CV: the sex $\times$ age $\times$ family-status intersection (women $\approx$30, the pregnancy window).

\section{What we built --- one pipeline, two domains (a third added to the design)}
Synthetic marker injection over \textbf{German Credit} (credit) and \textbf{synthetic CVs} (recruitment).
Synthetic substrates buy clean single-axis control and avoid the German-Credit sex$+$marital entanglement; a
Tier-1 structural-validation gate enforces single-slot, length-, and readability-matched pairs; a
\texttt{DomainSpec} registry makes adding a domain a one-entry change; and everything runs locally on Apple
Silicon (MLX). The result is a reusable matched-pair generator the whole team can build on.

The \textbf{new third domain --- education (grading student assignments/tests)} grades a student's essay,
with a strong/weak \texttt{high\_quality} label (thresholded from the essay's holistic score) as the quality
ground truth. It is built on \emph{real} essay corpora --- \textbf{PERSUADE 2.0} and \textbf{ASAP-AES} ---
held byte-identical, with a demographic marker injected only as a header (approach A1): a \textbf{name}
($\to$ sex $+$ ethnicity name-proxy, \citealp{haim2024whatsinaname}) and a \textbf{grade-level} cue ($\to$
age / education-stage). Crucially, essay quality is what RMs natively score, so this is the domain where
\crossinf{} became \emph{interpretable even on \modelSmall{}} (acc\_baseline 0.72 ASAP / 0.88 PERSUADE,
clearing the 0.6 bar that credit 0.51 / CV 0.59 failed) --- see Results.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Direct scoring (auto-influence)}
The bias is \textbf{large and low-complexity} and \textbf{replicates across both credit and CV}: sex and
intersection auto-influence $\approx$1.0, dropping to $\approx$0 when nulled
(sex on CV: \autoInflSexCVbase{}$\to$\autoInflSexCVnull{}). The intersection is only \emph{partially}
additive relative to the sum of its marginals (a genuine interaction component). A CV-specific twist:
\textbf{proxy $>$ explicit} for age and family status --- the RM reacts to realistic cues (graduation year,
career gap) more than to bald statements, the more concerning pattern.

\subsection{Cross-influence (the headline metric) --- and an honest limit}
\crossinf{} is the reliability-harm headline, but on the small \modelSmall{} RM it is \textbf{not cleanly
interpretable}: the RM barely tracks applicant quality (\texttt{acc\_baseline} $\approx$0.51 credit /
$\approx$0.59 CV), so the strong/weak label is not a valid ground truth. The built-in
\texttt{baseline\_tracks\_quality} flag caught this --- an honest negative that motivates scaling to a larger
RM.

\subsection{Modelling the actual model response (decision-response arm)}
A genuine USER-task $\to$ ASSISTANT-\emph{verdict} conversation, scored with each RM's real chat template:
does the RM reward a \textbf{discriminatory} verdict over a \textbf{fair} one? It resists overt
\textbf{sex/age} discrimination, but the margin \textbf{erodes on family-status / intersection} (the
pregnancy axis), and it never rewards an \textbf{evasive} non-answer over a substantive fair one.

\subsection{The reasoning discovery}
A correctness $\times$ conclusion \textbf{2$\times$2}, with a non-demographic \emph{commute} control, shows
the RM rewards \textbf{factually-correct causal reasoning} (correctness effect \reasonCorrectnessPL{}) plus a
positivity bias --- \textbf{not fairness per se} --- and the effect is \textbf{domain-general} (essentially
identical on the neutral control). This reframes the decision-response result: verdict scores are dominated
by reasoning quality and positivity, not demographic fairness.

\subsection{Method --- \leace{} and \taco{}: a stronger complexity test}
The base paper classifies a bias by whether sweeping null-space projection on a \diffmean{} direction makes
the behaviour drop cheaply. That only probes the \emph{linear} subspace and only asks whether nulling kills
the \emph{behaviour} --- not whether the concept is still \emph{present}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\leace{}} \citep{belrose2023leace}: closed-form, provably-\emph{optimal linear} concept
        erasure --- no linear classifier can recover the concept afterwards (a principled upgrade over
        heuristic \diffmean{} nulling).
  \item \textbf{\taco{} test} \citep{taco2024}: after optimal linear erasure, train a \emph{non-linear} (MLP)
        probe. Still recoverable $\Rightarrow$ ``linearly erasable but non-linearly recoverable'' $=$ the
        signature of an \textbf{entangled / high-complexity} bias.
\end{itemize}
This matters for us and the base paper: the nulling test can be \emph{fooled} --- a bias can look
low-complexity while the concept remains fully present in the representation. \leace{}$+$\taco{} is a
stronger, provable robustness check we can retrofit to the base paper's headline complexity claims.

\subsection{Applying \leace{}/\taco{} (the mechanistic headline)}
Run on the reasoning concepts (correctness, conclusion), held-out and paraphrase-diversified: after \leace{}
the \emph{linear} probe drops to chance (as guaranteed), but the \emph{MLP} still recovers at
\erasureMlpLeaceCorrPL{} ($\approx$99--100\%) $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{entangled / high-complexity}. So
null-space projection removes the \emph{reward effect}, not the \emph{represented concept} --- this corrects
the naive ``low-complexity'' reading and is a real safety point: \textbf{debiasing the reward $\neq$
debiasing the model}. Caveat: \modelSmall{} only; templated verdicts leave a residual surface confound.

\subsection{Education (grading) --- first results on real essays}
Built and run on \modelSmall{} over \textbf{ASAP-AES} and \textbf{PERSUADE 2.0} (A1 header proxies).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two large, low-complexity biases replicate across both corpora.} Explicit \textbf{sex}
        auto-influence 0.88 (ASAP) / 0.95 (PERSUADE) --- but the name proxy is only 0.24 / 0.35 (for sex,
        explicit $\gg$ proxy). And the \textbf{grade/stage} proxy (``7th-grade pupil'' vs ``final-year
        university student'') 0.96 / 0.97 --- while \emph{stated} age is near-zero (0.37 / 0.03): the RM
        rewards the identical essay far more when attributed to a university student. Both collapse under the
        $\alpha$-sweep. \textbf{Ethnicity} is weak and template-direction-unstable on both corpora.
  \item \textbf{\crossinf{} is interpretable} (acc\_baseline 0.72 / 0.88 $>0.6$), the metric credit/CV could
        not give on \modelSmall{} --- but CI\_base $\approx$ CI\_ref everywhere $\Rightarrow$ a symmetric
        marker main-effect, \emph{not} a protected-attribute-specific reliability harm at this scale.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positioned-argument (A2) --- standpoint-credibility bias}
Same real PERSUADE essay + argument, swap only the \emph{author's claimed identity} in a first-person
positionality sentence at the conclusion; decompose each axis (against a no-positionality baseline and
non-demographic controls) into a \emph{main effect} and an \emph{identity gap}. Result on \modelSmall{}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{General sycophancy for lived experience:} adding \emph{any} ``I have lived this'' appeal
        raises reward (main effect $+0.14$ to $+0.38$), even for hobby/pet personas.
  \item \textbf{A genuine, demographic-specific pro-marginalized bias:} the identity gap is large on the
        demographic axes (sex $+0.20$, race $+0.22$, class $+0.36$, origin $+0.28$, \textbf{intersection
        $+0.70$} for a working-class Black woman vs an affluent white man) while the clean neutral controls
        are $\approx 0$ (gardener-vs-cyclist $-0.01$, dog-vs-cat $+0.02$). The RM over-rewards the identical
        argument when voiced from a marginalized standpoint.
  \item \textbf{Caution:} auto-influence saturates (direction, not magnitude); we report the identity-gap
        magnitude against clean controls.
  \item \textbf{Robust to placement, corpus, phrasing, and stance.} The gap holds at every injection
        position (conclusion/opening/middle/random), replicates on ASAP (intersection $0.93$), survives three
        paraphrasings, and --- critically --- \textbf{persists under a \emph{neutral} (non-committal)
        standpoint} (intersection $0.70\to0.74$): the RM credits the marginalised \emph{standpoint itself},
        not merely a marginalised author who confidently \emph{agrees}. Clean controls stay $\approx0$
        throughout. (A ``disagree'' control is incoherent on these one-sided essays --- EDA-confirmed.)
\end{itemize}

\section{Decisions this cycle}
\label{sec:decisions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{All-in on Direction~1} (intersectional protected-attribute bias in high-risk decisions).
        Candidate Directions 2--5 (dialect/register, political content-vs-style, generational register, and
        the open slot) are parked; they reuse the same design grammar and can be revived later.
  \item \textbf{Third domain --- education (grading), built \& run.} A direct-scoring parity arm via the
        domain registry, on \emph{real} essay corpora (PERSUADE 2.0 $+$ ASAP-AES; A1 header proxies) rather
        than synthetic answers --- reflecting a preference for established datasets. Axes: name $\to$
        sex / ethnicity, grade-level $\to$ age. Delivered the interpretable \crossinf{} metric (see Results).
\end{itemize}

\section{Next steps}

\end{itemize}
